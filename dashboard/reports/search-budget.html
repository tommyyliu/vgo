Soft resignation plan

IdeasNot a plan yet, 2 Nov 2026

Where should search go?

Soft resignation moves search away from games that look decided. The general question is bigger: which positions deserve more search, and which deserve less? This report collects what we know and what to think about when we return to it.

In short

  • We can't yet measure what an extra simulation is worth, so this stays a list of ideas.
  • The fresh run will record the per-ply data we need, at almost no cost.
  • The first real step is a small experiment: search the same positions at several budgets and see what changes.
  • The biggest trap is letting a net that is confidently wrong decide where search goes.

Why this isn't a plan yet

Three things are missing:

  • No metric. Nothing measures how much a search changed the result, or how much it taught the net.
  • No per-ply data. Old games kept only fixed rule outcomes, and root values were thrown away.
  • A value head we can't lean on. On the 38 board, root values past 0.999 still picked the wrong side about 18% of the time at 128 input, though weak tail play may account for part of that. A net with no blocks at all matched the full net on held-out value sign, at 72.1% against 72.7%.

Any budget built today would rest on the number we trust least.

  • Soft resignation exists because bots stall. Earlier runs found them playing on even on completely settled boards.
  • Search only helps where the CPU can feed it. Here the card is the limit for the current net. On 8 vCPU the CPU is close to the limit already, and moving search around won't change that.
  • Full and fast turns already exist. --full-search-fraction mixes budgets and keeps only full searches as training samples. The first pilot's recipe was rejected, but that doesn't rule the idea out.

Two different questions

"How much can the next moves change the result?" is the right question for playing well. Self-play has a second job, which is teaching the net. A position can score high on one and low on the other, so a budget should consider both.

Where compute can move

Budget decisions happen at three levels. Soft resignation touches only the middle one.

Across games

  • Board mix and game counts
  • Komi and opening spread
  • Branching from contested positions

Across plies

  • The cheap tail
  • Mixed full and fast turns
  • A budget that follows the position

Inside one search

  • Stopping early when the top move can't be caught
  • Candidate widening
  • Tree reuse, parked for now

Signals we could use

Each signal reads something different, and none is proven. The fresh run records the three marked yes.

Contested area is the one I'd watch most. It connects this thread to the ownership head, and it's a measure of what's still up for grabs that doesn't depend on the value head.

Shapes a budget could take

Today's rule is a narrow notch at the extremes: 0.99 on the value scale is a 99.5% win chance. A budget could also step down in stages, or follow outcome uncertainty smoothly.

A random share of full-budget searches belongs in any of these shapes. Without one, a net's confident mistakes stop getting checked.

Traps

  • The net is least reliable where it's surest. A budget that follows its confidence can starve exactly the positions it's wrong about.
  • Training data shifts. If budget decides which positions get strong targets, the training mix changes with it, and the net drifts toward what it already searched.
  • Targets vary in quality. A policy target from 200 visits is noisier than one from 1000. Thinning or weighting has to account for that, and our importance-corrected policy target depends on the candidates sampled.
  • Speed can be spent twice. Budget savings overlap with faster nets, and both run into the same CPU limit.
  • Arena settings can drift from self-play. Decide early whether a budget applies only in self-play. Rating must stay comparable.
  • Loss curves won't settle it. Compare at matched compute, on both boards, with about 500 games.

When we come back

The time to return is when the fresh run has a few thousand games and the tail is stable. Then, in order:

  1. Look at the recorded signals

    Which ones predict the result changing later in the game? Which predict the net being surprised by search? Split by board and stage.

  2. Search the same positions at several budgets

    Take a sample of positions and search each at 400, 1000 and 4000 simulations. How often does the chosen move change, and how far does value move? This is the most direct measure of what extra search buys, and it needs only a checkpoint and existing tools.

  3. Pick a metric

    Use steps 1 and 2 to choose what the budget should follow, and write down why.

  4. Pilot one shape

    Try the simplest budget that follows that metric, with a random share of full searches, and test it at matched compute.
