# Restart experiments: production, model cost, and search budgets

This continues [the first restart measurements](RESTART_2026-10-13.md).
Recipes are in [`scripts/experiments/restart-2026-10-14.py`](../../scripts/experiments/restart-2026-10-14.py).
Raw outputs live under `artifacts/experiments-2026-10-14/`; `runs.jsonl`
records each command, exit code, elapsed time and executable digest. Experiments
run sequentially on the local Ryzen 9950X / RTX 5070 Ti, without competing
training or compilations during benchmarks.

## 1. Spatial pruning under the production search recipe

The paired neural benchmark uses the 256-input checkpoint, 128 policy grid,
32 actors, 1,000 simulations, leaf batch four, coarse pool 16, widening six,
321 candidates, two TensorRT fp16 sessions, batch capacity 32 and root noise
0.1. Every actor performs a fresh search on a saved played position. Each board
group covers all 16 fixtures twice. One warm-up pass precedes three timed passes;
reported throughput uses median batch elapsed time divided by 32. Broker
counters include the warm-up. Spread is the timed-pass range divided by median.

The baseline build has identical instrumentation and dependencies, with only
`crates/vgo-core/src/legal_set.rs` restored to HEAD. Build output is separate
under `/tmp/vgo-production-baseline-target`; preserve the benchmark baseline
source if reproducing after the pruning change is committed. Run order is before/after followed
by after/before. This measures neural MCTS throughput, including encoding and
broker scheduling, but excludes gameplay progression and replay publication.

An initial 240-second continuous production probe is retained at
`production/p0-before/`. It completed only two games (99 published samples),
while searching 1,257 plies. Starting every actor from an empty board makes it
poor evidence for mature-position CPU costs or sustained completed-game rate;
the saved-position test supplies that coverage. Interrupted games are discarded.

| Board | Before searches/s (two runs) | After searches/s (two runs) | Change in mean |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 18 | 4.820, 4.571 | 4.813, 4.699 | +1.3% |
| 38 | 3.290, 3.220 | 3.340, 3.454 | +4.4% |

Big-board mean encoding cost drops from about 3,421 to 2,979 µs per
position (12.9%); average batch fill improves from about 14.7 to 16.2 positions.
Every run reports zero inference failures. Each big-board pair improves, but
by different amounts (+1.5% and +7.3%); two pairs do not provide a narrow
uncertainty estimate. The small-board result is near timing variability.

**Decision:** retain the equivalent indexed pruning. It saves measurable CPU
work and gives a modest big-board neural-search gain under the production
recipe. Do not interpret the raster gain as a proportional increase in games/h
or learning strength/h. The earlier matched continuous probe and this played-
position test support the change without establishing a long-run training gain.

## 2. Model experiments

### Matched resolution baseline

The existing `artifacts/res-ab/r128-w150k.pt` and `r256-w150k.pt` reports
contain the same ordered 853 dataset digests and 150,017 samples. Both use a
width-64, 16-block trunk, one context attention block, eight norm groups, eight
epochs, batch 64 and learning rate 0.0005. Their seed is 424242.

| Input size | Optimization seconds | Whole training update seconds |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 128 | 424.31 | 531.77 |
| 256 | 1,770.22 | 2,013.55 |

These are historical single runs, not repeated timing estimates. Optimization
was 4.17 times slower at 256. Existing equal-simulation arena results for the
128 candidate are 37–43 on the small board (95% interval 35.7–57.1%) and 18–22
on the big board (30.7–60.2%). Both are inconclusive.

### One normalization per residual block

Train from scratch on that exact frozen corpus and recipe at resolution 128,
removing only the second GroupNorm in each convolutional residual block.
Attention and head norms are unchanged. Checkpoints/export sidecars record
`norms_per_block`; missing metadata defaults to two for old checkpoints.
The corpus is frozen using symlinks to the original games; dataset digests,
sample counts and game-grouped validation counts must match before comparison.

Corpus parity passed: 853 ordered digests, 150,017 total rows, 132,515 training
and 17,502 validation rows. The packed export advertises its actual three inputs
and the one-norm count. Training optimization took 413.56 seconds versus
424.31 for the historical two-norm baseline (2.5% less), but the complete update
took 564.45 versus 531.77 seconds. Startup/compilation costs differ; this is
not strong evidence of faster training. Sampled trainer RSS peaked around
9.3 GiB, with total card memory around 3.4 GiB during the observed epochs.

Final validation policy KL is 1.5900 versus 1.5876; value MAE is 0.3157 versus
0.3218. No divergence was observed. These losses support proceeding to arenas,
not an adoption decision.

### Strength and deployment cost

Fresh matches use 24 color-swapped pairs per board, 16 concurrent games, komi
`28 / units²`, and the normal size-scaled cap. Compare both equal simulations
(200) and equal advisory time (200 ms, simulation ceiling 10,000). No root
exploration noise is used in arena play. A pending broker round may overrun the
deadline; reports include actual search time and simulation counts for both
seats. Saved-position neural benchmarks use the same production search recipe.

Small matches can detect a large failure but do not establish equivalence.
Each experimental variant requires an adopt/reject/inconclusive decision;
validation loss and raw speed do not substitute for playing-strength evidence.

Saved-position neural throughput (32 actors × 1,000 simulations):

| Model | Small-board searches/s | Big-board searches/s |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| 256, two norms (mean of paired after runs) | 4.756 | 3.397 |
| 128, two norms | 17.337 | 6.668 |
| 128, one norm | 18.823 | 6.957 |

128 is about 3.65× faster on small fixtures and 1.96× on big fixtures. One norm
adds about 8.6% and 4.3% respectively relative to the 128 baseline. The 128
variants each have one benchmark run with three timed passes; their low within-
run spreads do not measure between-run variability. Tree shape also changes
with model predictions, so these are deployment costs rather than isolated
normalization-kernel timings. All report zero inference failures, including
the one-norm model served through TensorRT fp16.

Fresh 128-versus-256 equal-time results:

| Board | 128 wins–losses | Score | Wilson 95% interval | Mean simulations, 128 / 256 |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- | ---: |
| 18 | 24–24 | 50.0% | 36.4–63.6% | 143 / 81 |
| 38 | 30–18 | 62.5% | 48.4–74.8% | 88 / 47 |

Both matches exited cleanly with zero inference failures. Mean searches take
203–205 ms for 128 and 207–210 ms for 256; longest searches are 211–220 and
221–231 ms respectively. These actual times make the advisory-deadline overrun
visible. The matches measure two models sharing this machine at 16 concurrent
games, not dedicated-device single-game latency. Intervals are the arena's
per-game Wilson estimates, not a paired bootstrap or an equivalence test.

**Resolution decision: inconclusive on strength; compelling on cost.** The
big-board result favors 128 but its interval still includes 50%. Retain the
existing campaign default, and use 128 as the leading candidate for a new
efficiency-focused campaign. A larger replication at the intended thinking
budget is the next strength test. Changing resolution requires a matching
checkpoint/export; an existing 256-input model cannot simply be rendered at 128.

Fresh one-norm-versus-two-norm results (128 inputs, candidate is one norm):

| Board | Budget | Wins–losses | Score | Wilson 95% interval |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| 18 | 200 simulations | 22–26 | 45.8% | 32.6–59.7% |
| 18 | 200 ms | 18–30 | 37.5% | 25.2–51.6% |
| 38 | 200 simulations | 18–30 | 37.5% | 25.2–51.6% |
| 38 | 200 ms | 28–20 | 58.3% | 44.3–71.2% |

All matches exit cleanly and report zero inference failures. In the big-board
timed match one norm averages 112 simulations versus 99, with both seats around
205 ms. At fixed simulations it averages 601 ms versus 610 ms, a much smaller
speed difference than the saved-position benchmark. Model predictions change
which positions the games reach and which branches get searched.

**Normalization decision: inconclusive; retain two norms.** One norm trains
and serves without divergence, but the modest speed gain does not yield a
consistent strength benefit in these pilots. Results are at one training seed
and relatively shallow search budgets; they do not rule out one norm with a
different training recipe. Numeric serving parity is checked separately.

The serving probe samples 24 real replay positions (opening, middle and final
rows from four games per board), with three repeated inference calls. The first
attempt failed in the probe's own CPU/GPU table placement. A second attempt
used Python provider options in the native API's numeric boolean form; Python
rejected them and silently fell back to CUDA. That output is retained separately
and is not TensorRT fp16 evidence. The corrected probe requires an active
TensorRT provider and passed before the corrected generation runs. Native fp16
benchmarks and arenas already pass their finite-output checks. Numeric parity
is a sampled accuracy check, not a proof over all positions or a substitute for
playing-strength measurements.

Verified TensorRT parity: maximum value error is below 0.0006 for both models;
maximum policy total variation is below 0.003; repeated outputs are identical.
`serving-parity.json` records actual deltas, provider lists and model digests.

## 3. Randomized search-budget pilot

The initial fixed-arm attempt passed `--profile-seconds` without a profile
output, which the old generator silently ignored. It was deliberately stopped
and excluded, with its data retained under `failed-budget-games/fixed-unbounded`.
The experiment now uses the explicit `--run-seconds 600` control. A CPU smoke
run without the profiling feature verifies cancellation after about one second;
CLI tests cover independent and combined deadlines. Misconfigured profile
deadlines now fail before CUDA initialization. The measured arms use the
corrected control, without sampling-profiler overhead.

The corrected fixed arm reaches its deadline with 32 published games and 3,930
rows, then exits nonzero solely because the new limit's intentional cancellation
was still handled by the old profile-only exit exception. Its final telemetry
reports zero inference failures. Every published dataset hash, sample count
and per-ply eligibility record was checked and reconciles with telemetry;
`fixed-deadline-acceptance.json` records why this corpus is accepted despite
the preserved exit status. It is not clean-exit evidence. The cancellation path
now recognizes only the explicit generation-cancelled sentinel after a deadline,
counts interrupted games, and leaves genuine backend errors fatal. A slow-search
CPU smoke run exercises cancellation inside a game and exits successfully.

Both arms generate for 600 seconds using the 128 incumbent, 32 actors, the
production 50% big / 25% small / 25% intermediate board mix, 1,000 full
simulations, and the existing 0.99 soft resignation discount to 400 simulations.
The fixed arm selects full search every turn. The randomized arm selects full
search with probability 0.25 (first ply always full), otherwise 200 simulations
without root noise or temperature. Soft resignation reduces either budget;
it never increases the fast budget. Seeds and all other controls match.

Only full-selected rows train **both policy and value**, at the existing loss
weights. Fast turns affect play and the final value label and still enter
resignation calibration; dropping them from replay must not drop them from
calibration. Each game's sidecar records actual budgets and training eligibility
by ply. Search telemetry counts eligible turns separately from total turns;
only complete games are published. Cancellation discards remaining partial games.

Each arm trains from scratch for eight epochs on its published corpus using
the baseline two-norm recipe. This tests equal generation time, followed by
training each resulting dataset; training cost is recorded separately. It is
one bounded learning update, not a multi-generation strength-per-hour trial.
Fresh equal-simulation and equal-time matches compare the resulting models.
More games alone is not a strength result, and the first-ply rule slightly raises
the eligible fraction above 25%.

Measured generation outputs:

| Arm | Elapsed generation seconds | Complete games | Published eligible rows | Searched turns | Full-selected turns |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fixed | 603.88 | 32 | 3,930 | 8,921 | 8,921 |
| Randomized | 603.68 | 90 | 3,615 | 17,792 | 4,554 |

The randomized arm exits cleanly with zero inference failures. Per-ply metadata
reconciles with each published game's sample count. It publishes 2.81 times as
many games with 8.0% fewer training rows in this bounded window. About 79% of
its full-selected turns become published rows, versus 44% in the fixed arm;
the rest are in unfinished games discarded at the cutoff. This publication
advantage partly reflects cold startup and the bounded cutoff, and must not be
presented as a steady-state rows/hour improvement.

Publication mix is also different: fixed completes 14 small / 9 big / 9
intermediate games; randomized completes 26 / 45 / 19. Big-board rows rise from
2,096 to 2,502 while small-board rows fall from 845 to 436. Thus this pilot
changes both within-game correlation and the board distribution of published
training data. A resulting arena difference cannot be attributed solely to
less correlation. Full-selected rows after the soft-resign trigger comprise
2,311/3,930 in fixed and 1,206/3,615 in randomized; these keep the existing
policy/value weights. This describes the known cheap-tail mitigation, rather
than reopening the longer-game experiment.

### Pilot learning and arena results

| Arm | Training / validation rows | Optimization seconds | Whole update seconds | Final value MAE |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fixed | 3,343 / 587 | 42.81 | 81.98 | 0.5264 |
| Randomized | 3,361 / 254 | 20.73 | 34.25 | 1.0041 |

Compilation/startup differ between sequential runs, so these are actual pipeline
costs, not repeated steady-state training estimates. Generation plus training
update takes about 686 seconds for fixed versus 638 for randomized, excluding
export. This is equal generation time followed by the same eight passes,
**not an exact equal-total-compute strength trial**.

The randomized student loses 0–48 in each of four fresh matches: small/big
boards at 200 simulations and at 200 ms. Each per-match Wilson interval is
0–7.4%; all runs exit cleanly with zero inference failures. Its inference value
head barely learns in this tiny from-scratch corpus (training value MAE 0.9870,
validation 1.0041), unlike the fixed student. Its validation policy KL is also
worse (2.2721 versus 1.8895), although losses across different corpora are not
directly comparable measures of strength.

Before attributing this to the algorithm, every replay row was checked against
its game: stored ply equals the full-selected eligibility indices, mover agrees
with ply, and mover-relative value equals the independently scored game outcome.
All checks pass. Positive/negative value counts are 1,967/1,963 for fixed and
1,790/1,825 for randomized. This rules out the suspected skipped-row label
misalignment or a one-class dataset. Evidence is in `budget-label-alignment.json`.

**Decision: reject adoption of this pilot recipe; retain fixed budgets.** More
complete games is established for this bounded slice, but the trained student
is unusable. This does not establish that randomized budgets fail in the normal
150k-sample bulk regime: both pilot corpora are tiny, the published board mix
differs, there is one seed and one update, and total pipeline times differ.
A follow-up below blends each arm into the same larger replay window and checks
deployed value-head learning. Keep the new controls opt-in until a representative
learning comparison succeeds.

### Follow-up with a common full replay corpus

Both arms add their published pilot games to the exact same ordered 853-game,
150,017-row corpus used for the resolution and normalization experiments. Train
from scratch with the normal two-norm, 128-input recipe for eight epochs.
The resulting corpus sizes are 153,947 and 153,632 rows; only about 2.5% is
new experience, so this is a small update, not the usual 15% turnover or a
multi-generation trial. The common ordered digests are checked explicitly.

The training budgets are 16,984 versus 16,992 optimizer steps: one extra partial
batch per epoch, a difference below 0.05%. Actual elapsed pipeline time is also
reported; matching nominal work does not force identical wall time. Fresh
48-game equal-time matches on each board use the same 200-ms advisory budget.
The normal replay regime must learn usable deployed value heads before its
strength results can support any budget decision.

| Arm | Train / validation rows | Optimization seconds | Whole update seconds | Final value MAE |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Fixed + common replay | 135,858 / 18,089 | 466.15 | 600.43 | 0.3184 |
| Randomized + common replay | 135,876 / 17,756 | 466.58 | 593.96 | 0.3530 |

Both value heads learn; the tiny-corpus failure does not persist here. The
generation-plus-update totals are 1,204.31 and 1,197.63 seconds (within 0.6%,
excluding export); optimization time differs by about 0.09%. The initially
running supervisor required exactly equal batch counts and stopped after both
successful exports. The pre-arena verification accepts and records the known
one-partial-batch difference per epoch, while requiring identical common-corpus
digests. No model or report counts were altered to satisfy that check.

| Board | Randomized-window wins–losses | Score | Wilson 95% interval |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| 18 | 28–20 | 58.3% | 44.3–71.2% |
| 38 | 26–22 | 54.2% | 40.3–67.4% |

Both matches exit cleanly with zero inference failures. Search averages are
about 203 ms on small boards and 205 ms on big boards for both seats. The
randomized-window model averages 167 versus 155 simulations on the small board,
and 93 versus 91 on the big board; these are measured workloads, not assumed
simulation equality.

**Full-window decision: inconclusive on strength; keep the controls opt-in.**
The near-equal pipeline-cost update learns a usable student and its point
estimates favor randomized data on both boards. Both intervals still include
50%, and neither establishes equivalent or improved strength. This prevents
the tiny-corpus failure from being mistaken for a general rejection of budget
randomization. A larger-turnover, multiple-seed or multiple-update trial is the
next adoption test, with board mix and partial-game publication reported.

## Verification and artifacts

The relevant Rust search/self-play tests and Python learner, checkpoint,
manifest and model tests pass. Deadline-specific regressions cover independent
run limits, combined limits, and genuine backend errors at a deadline; CPU
smokes verify both ordinary timed exit and mid-game cancellation without the
profiling feature. Real TensorRT benchmarks, export parity and all twelve fresh
arena matches are recorded alongside their process statuses.

`summary.json` contains the completed results. `runs.jsonl` preserves failed
attempts as well as successful ones; the fixed-arm acceptance exception is
explicit and must not be read as a clean process exit. The unbounded first run
and CUDA fallback probe are excluded from the reported comparison. No bulk
campaign has been launched and experiment settings remain opt-in.
